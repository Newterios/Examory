# Supplement S3. Where do the positive cases come from? A scoping sample of 48 studies

**Question.** In peer-reviewed studies (2015–2026) that propose or evaluate detectors of cheating in online quizzes, examinations or course assessments from log or response data, where do the cheating cases used for evaluation come from?

**Scope.** Included: detectors that use response times, clickstream, answer changes, scores, answer similarity, IP addresses or timestamps. Excluded: webcam or video proctoring, plagiarism of essays or code, keystroke or other biometrics for identity, and studies that evaluate no detector.

**Search.** Non-systematic scoping search on 15 November 2026: general web search, Europe PMC title queries for 2015–2026 restricted to eight journals, Crossref title look-ups, and backward and forward snowballing from anchor studies. Texts were read on publisher sites, PubMed Central, arXiv, author post-prints and conference proceedings.

**Coding.** Each study has one primary category:

| Code | Positive cases are… |
|---|---|
| R-conf | real cases confirmed independently of the detector (investigation, admission, proctor report) |
| R-exp | cheating induced experimentally in real participants |
| R-proxy | real data labelled by a heuristic, by another detector, or by a score difference |
| S-inject | synthetic: feature values or score sequences of real or simulated records altered by a rule |
| S-mech | synthetic: generated by a simulation of the cheating process (psychometric simulation of pre-knowledge or copying counts here) |
| none | no labelled positives; only inspection or description of flagged cases |

Confidence: **H**, the methods or abstract text that states the provenance was read; **M**, inferred from the abstract only. Studies that could not be accessed were not counted.

**Limitations.** This is a scoping sample by a single coder, without a registered protocol, a second rater or a bibliographic-database search; the counts describe the sample, not the literature. Open-access journals are over-represented in the psychometric group. Two research programmes contribute several entries each. Five of the six R-conf studies use one credentialing data set whose flags came partly from statistical analysis, so that category is optimistic. Overlap judgements rest on method descriptions, not on code.

## Earlier statements of the circularity problem

- Belov and Toton (2022, B4) state that validation on fully simulated data, or on real data with added simulated aberrance, is biased because the assumptions about the aberrance enter both the detector and the simulated data.
- Gorney and Wollack (2022, *J. Educational Measurement* 59(1), 22–42, doi:10.1111/jedm.12309) show that the model used to generate item pre-knowledge changes the measured power of detection methods.
- Zopluoglu (2019, B6) notes that a model trained on simulated cheaters depends on how realistic the simulated behaviour is.

No study in group A was found that names the overlap between heuristic labels and detector inputs as a problem; the closest are caveats by the authors of A4 and A13 about their own labels. This is an absence in a scoping sample, not evidence that no such paper exists.

## Notable exclusions

Trezise et al. (2019; writing tasks), Bawarith et al. (2017; fingerprint and eye tracking), webcam and video detectors, Zimmermann et al. (2016; paper-based test, item-level leak detection), Bilen and Matros (2021; descriptive evidence, no detector evaluation), and several studies whose label provenance could not be read (among them Garg and Goel, 2023; the chapters of the Cizek and Wollack handbook).
